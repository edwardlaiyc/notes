4 Inverse of a Matrix

If AA is an invertible n×nn\times n matrix, then for each 𝒙\mathbf{x} in ℝn\mathbb{R}^n, the equation A𝒙=𝒃A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} has the unique solution 𝒙=A−1𝒃\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b} Proof

  1. Show that solution exists
    Substitute A−1𝒃A^{-1}\mathbf{b} into A𝒙=𝒃A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b},
    LHS = A𝒙=A(A−1)𝒃=(AA−1)𝒃=I𝒃=𝒃A\mathbf{x}=A(A^{-1})\mathbf{b}=(AA^{-1})\mathbf{b}=I\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{b} = RHS.

  2. Show that solution is unique.
    Suppose there is some vector uu such that A𝒖=𝒃A\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{b},
    then A−1A𝒖=A−1𝒃⇒I𝒖=A−1𝒃⇒𝒖=A−1𝒃A^{-1}A\mathbf{u}=A^{-1}\mathbf{b} \Rightarrow I\mathbf{u}=A^{-1}\mathbf{b} \Rightarrow \mathbf{u}=A^{-1}\mathbf{b}.

Property: If AA is invertible, then A−1A^{-1} is invertible, and (A−1)−1=A(A^{-1})^{-1}=A

Property: If AA and BB are n×nn\times n invertible matrices, then so is ABAB and (AB)−1=B−1A−1(AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1}

Proof
To find some matrix CC s.t. (AB)C=I(AB)C=I and C(AB)=I.C(AB)=I.
If C=B−1A−1C=B^{-1}A^{-1}, then AB(B−1A−1)=A(BB−1)A−1=AIA−1=AA−1=IAB(B^{-1}A^{-1})=A(BB^{-1})A^{-1}=AIA^{-1}=AA^{-1}=I.
Similar argument applies for (B−1A−1)(AB)=I(B^{-1}A^{-1})(AB)=I.

Property: If AA is an invertible matrix, then so is ATA^T, and (AT)−1=(A−1)T(A^T)^{-1}=(A^{-1})^T Proof
To show that (A−1)T(A^{-1})^T is the inverse of ATA^T.
Considering the product of ATA^T and (A−1)T(A^{-1})^T, AT(A−1)T=(A−1A)T=IT=IA^T(A^{-1})^T=(A^{-1}A)^T=I^T=I and the product of (A−1)T(A^{-1})^T and ATA^T, (A−1)TAT=(AA−1)T=IT=I(A^{-1})^TA^T=(AA^{-1})^T=I^T=I

4.1 Elementary matrix

An elementary matrix EE is created by performing exactly one elementary row operation on an identity matrix II.
Multiplying a matrix AA by EE on the left (EAEA) performs that same row operation on AA.

For example, E=[010100001] and A=[abcdefghi]⇒EA=[defabcghi]E = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \text{ and }A=\begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & i \end{bmatrix}\Rightarrow EA=\begin{bmatrix} d & e & f \\ a & b & c \\ g & h & i \end{bmatrix} Every elementary matrix is invertible, and the inverse is another elementary matrix that reverses the original transformation. For example, E1=[010100001]⇒E1−1=[010100001]E_1=\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \Rightarrow E_1^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} E2=[100010−401]⇒E2−1=[100010401]E_2=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -4 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \Rightarrow E_2^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}

An n×nn\times n matrix AA is invertible iff AA is row equivalent to InI_n. The same sequence of EROs that reduces AA to InI_n transforms InI_n to A−1A^{-1}.

Proof
Suppose AA is an invertible n×nn\times n matrix, then A𝒙=𝒃A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} has a unique solution for any 𝒃\mathbf{b}. This means the RREF form of AA has a pivot in every row and column. For a n×nn\times n matrix, this has to be InI_n. Therefore AA is row equivalent to InI_n.
There exists some sequence of EROs that transforms AA to InI_n, and each row operation corresponds to multiplication by an elementary matrix. This can be expressed as (Ek⋯E2E1)A=In(E_k\cdots E_2E_1)A=I_n AA is invertible since RHS=InI_n is invertible and the product of elementary matrices is invertible.
Multiplying by A−1A^{-1} on the right on both sides, (Ek⋯E2E1)=A−1(E_k\cdots E_2E_1)=A^{-1} Therefore, (Ek⋯E2E1)In=A−1(E_k\cdots E_2E_1)I_n=A^{-1}

4.1.2 Invertible Matrix Theorem

Let AA be an n×nn\times n matrix. Then the following statements are logically equivalent:

  1. AA is an invertible (non-singular) matrix.

  2. AA is row equivalent to InI_n.

  3. AA has nn pivot positions.

  4. A𝒙=𝟎A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} has only the trivial solution.

  5. The columns of AA form a linearly independent set.

  6. A𝒙=𝒃A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} has at least one (in fact, unique) solution for each 𝒃\mathbf{b} in ℝn\mathbb{R}^n.

  7. The columns of AA span ℝn\mathbb{R}^n.

  8. There is an n×nn\times n matrix CC such that CA=ICA=I.

  9. There is an n×nn\times n matrix DD such that AD=IAD=I.

  10. ATA^T is an invertible matrix.

  11. The columns of AA form a basis for ℝn\mathbb{R}^n.

  12. 𝑪(A)=ℝn\mathbf{C}(A) = \mathbb{R}^n.

  13. dim⁡𝑪(A)=n\dim \mathbf{C}(A) = n.

  14. Rank of A=nA = n.

  15. 𝑵(A)={𝟎}\mathbf{N}(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}.

  16. dim⁡𝑵(A)=0\dim \mathbf{N}(A) = 0.

5 Matrix Factorisation

To solve sequence of equations like Ax=b1,Ax=b2,…,Ax=bpAx=b_1, Ax=b_2,\ldots, Ax=b_p, it is inefficient to first compute the inverse A−1A^{-1} and then multiply it by each vector, A−1b1,...,A−1bpA^{-1}b_{1}, ..., A^{-1}b_{p}.

Instead, the efficient solution is to decompose Am×nA_{m \times n} into Lm×mUm×nL_{m \times m}U_{m \times n}.

L=[10⋯0l211⋯0⋮⋮⋱⋮lm1lm2⋯1],U=[u11u12⋯u1n0u22⋯u2n⋮⋮⋱⋮00⋯umn]L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ l_{21} & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ l_{m1} & l_{m2} & \cdots & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{bmatrix} u_{11} & u_{12} & \cdots & u_{1n} \\ 0 & u_{22} & \cdots & u_{2n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & u_{mn} \end{bmatrix} where LL is unit lower triangular matrix, UU is upper triangular matrix.

5.1 Deriving L & U

Assumption AA can be reduced to echelon form without requiring any row interchanges.

First reduce matrix AA to UU, using elementary row operations. Multiply AA by a sequence of unit lower triangular elementary matrices (E1,…,EpE_{1}, \dots, E_{p}), such that

Ep…E1A=U⟹A=(Ep…E1)−1U\begin{align*} & E_{p} \dots E_{1}A = U \\ \implies & A = (E_{p} \dots E_{1})^{-1}U \end{align*} Then L=Ep…E1L = E_{p} \dots E_{1}.

Example Consider a 3×33 \times 3 matrix: A=[𝟐−23𝟔−714𝟒−830]A = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{2} & -2 & 3 \\ \mathbf{6} & -7 & 14 \\ \mathbf{4} & -8 & 30 \end{bmatrix}

Step 1: Row Reduction to find UU. Apply elementary row operations to eliminate the entries below the pivots to reach echelon form.

First Column: The first pivot is the 𝟐\mathbf{2} in row 1, column 1. Eliminate the entries below it. Multiply by E21E_{21} (which represents R2−3R1R_2 - 3R_1): E21A=[2−230−𝟏54−830]E_{21}A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -2 & 3 \\ 0 & \mathbf{-1} & 5 \\ 4 & -8 & 30 \end{bmatrix} Multiply by E31E_{31} (which represents R3−2R1R_3 - 2R_1): E31(E21A)=[2−230−𝟏50−𝟒24]E_{31}(E_{21}A) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -2 & 3 \\ 0 & \mathbf{-1} & 5 \\ 0 & \mathbf{-4} & 24 \end{bmatrix}

Second Column: The new pivot is the −𝟏\mathbf{-1} in row 2, column 2. Eliminate the entry below it. * Multiply by E32E_{32} (which represents R3−4R2R_3 - 4R_2): E32(E31E21A)=[2−230−15004]=UE_{32}(E_{31}E_{21}A) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -2 & 3 \\ 0 & -1 & 5 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = U

Step 2: Two Methods to Construct LL

Since AA has 3 rows, LL will be a 3×33 \times 3 unit lower triangular matrix.

Method 1: The "Multiplier" Method (Fastest).

At each pivot column, divide the eliminated entries by the pivot for that column, and place the result directly into LL.

Column 1 Multipliers: The pivot is 𝟐\mathbf{2}. Divide 6 and 4 by 2 respectively.

Column 2 Multipliers: The new pivot is −𝟏\mathbf{-1}. Divide -4 by -1.

Placing these multipliers below the diagonal of 1s gives you LL: L=[100𝟑10𝟐𝟒1]L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \mathbf{3} & 1 & 0 \\ \mathbf{2} & \mathbf{4} & 1 \end{bmatrix}

Method 2: The Elementary Inverse Method. Mathematically, LL is formed by the inverse of the elementary matrices used to reach UU: L=E21−1E31−1E32−1L = E_{21}^{-1}E_{31}^{-1}E_{32}^{-1}.

Construct LL by simply taking the nonzero off-diagonal elements of the elementary inverse matrices and placing them into the appropriate positions in LL.

L=[100𝟑10001][100010𝟐01][1000100𝟒1]=[100𝟑10𝟐𝟒1]L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \mathbf{3} & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ \mathbf{2} & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \mathbf{4} & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \mathbf{3} & 1 & 0 \\ \mathbf{2} & \mathbf{4} & 1 \end{bmatrix}

5.3 Zero Pivots: PA=LUPA = LU Factorization

The standard A=LUA = LU factorization assumes matrix AA can be reduced to echelon form without row interchanges. If a pivot becomes zero, you must swap rows, which breaks the standard algorithm.

To solve this, we pre-swap the rows of AA using a Permutation Matrix PP, which is an Identity matrix with its rows swapped. This yields the generalized factorization: PA=LUPA = LU

Example: Given matrix AA: A=[11111−12−1−1]A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & -1 \\ 2 & -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}

If we attempt standard row reduction (R2−R1R_2 - R_1 and R3−2R1R_3 - 2R_1), we hit a zero pivot in the second column: [1110𝟎−20−3−3]\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & \mathbf{0} & -2 \\ 0 & -3 & -3 \end{bmatrix}

To fix this, we swap Row 2 and Row 3 using permutation matrix P23P_{23}: P=[100001010]P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}

Calculate PAPA to generate the row-swapped matrix: PA=[100001010][11111−12−1−1]=[1112−1−111−1]PA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & -1 \\ 2 & -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}

Now, factor PAPA normally into LL and UU: Using pivot 1 in column 1: R2−𝟐R1⟹l21=2R_2 - \mathbf{2}R_1 \implies l_{21} = 2, and R3−𝟏R1⟹l31=1R_3 - \mathbf{1}R_1 \implies l_{31} = 1. The entry below the second pivot (−3-3) is already 0⟹l32=00 \implies l_{32} = 0.

This gives our final LL and UU matrices for PAPA: L=[100210101],U=[1110−3−300−2]L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -3 & -3 \\ 0 & 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}
