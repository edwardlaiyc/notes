5 Variance

For a random variable XX, the variance is Var(X)=𝕍[X]=𝔼[(X−𝔼[X])2]=𝔼[X2]−(𝔼[X])2\begin{align*} \text{Var}(X)=\mathbb{V}[X] &= \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2] \\ &=\mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2 \end{align*}

Variance measures the average of the squared distances from the mean.

Standard deviation is σ=𝕍[X]\sigma = \sqrt{\mathbb{V}[X]}

For a constant cc,

  1. 𝕍[c]=0\mathbb{V}[c]=0
    ∵𝔼[(c−𝔼[c])2]=𝔼[(c−c)2]=𝔼[0]=0\because\mathbb{E}[(c-\mathbb{E}[c])^2]=\mathbb{E}[(c-c)^2]=\mathbb{E}[0]=0

  2. 𝕍[X+c]=𝕍[X]\mathbb{V}[X+c] = \mathbb{V}[X]
    ∵𝔼[((X+c)−𝔼[X+c])2]=𝔼[(X+c−(𝔼[X]+c))2]=𝔼[(X−𝔼[X])2]\because\mathbb{E}[((X+c)-\mathbb{E}[X+c])^2]=\mathbb{E}[(X+c-(\mathbb{E}[X]+c))^2]=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2]

  3. 𝕍[cX]=c2𝕍[X]\mathbb{V}[cX]=c^2\mathbb{V}[X]
    ∵𝔼[(cX−𝔼[cX])2]=𝔼[(cX−c𝔼[X])2]=𝔼[c2(X−𝔼[X])2]=c2𝔼[(X−𝔼[X])2]\because\mathbb{E}[(cX-\mathbb{E}[cX])^2]=\mathbb{E}[(cX-c\mathbb{E}[X])^2]=\mathbb{E}[c^2(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2]=c^2\mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2]

5.1 Covariance

For random variables XX, YY, Cov(X,Y)=𝔼[(X−𝔼[X])(Y−𝔼[Y])]=𝔼[XY]−𝔼[X]𝔼[Y]\begin{align*} \text{Cov}(X,Y)&=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y])] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[XY]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y] \end{align*}

Note that Variance is a specific case of Covariance, i.e. Cov(X,X)=𝕍[X]\text{Cov}(X,X)=\mathbb{V}[X]

For random variables XX, YY and constants c1c_1, c2c_2, 𝕍[c1X+c2Y]=c12𝕍[X]+c22𝕍[Y]+2c1c2𝔼[(X−𝔼[X])(Y−𝔼[Y])]=c12𝕍[X]+c22𝕍[Y]+2c1c2Cov(X,Y)\begin{align*} \mathbb{V}[c_1X+c_2Y]&=c_1^2\mathbb{V}[X]+c_2^2\mathbb{V}[Y]+2c_1c_2\mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y])] \\ &=c_1^2\mathbb{V}[X]+c_2^2\mathbb{V}[Y]+2c_1c_2\text{Cov}(X,Y) \end{align*}

Proof.
𝕍[c1X+c2Y]=𝔼[((c1X+c2Y)−(c1𝔼[X]+c2𝔼[Y]))2]=𝔼[(c1(X−𝔼[X])+c2(Y−𝔼[Y]))2]=𝔼[c12(X−𝔼[X])2]+𝔼[c22(Y−𝔼[Y])2]+2c1c2(X−𝔼[X])(Y−𝔼[Y])=c12𝕍[X]+c22𝕍[Y]+2c1c2Cov(X,Y)\begin{align*} \mathbb{V}[c_1X+c_2Y]&=\mathbb{E}[((c_1X+c_2Y)-(c_1\mathbb{E}[X]+c_2\mathbb{E}[Y]))^2] \\ &=\mathbb{E}[(c_1(X-\mathbb{E}[X])+c_2(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y]))^2] \\ &=\mathbb{E}[c_1^2(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2]+\mathbb{E}[c_2^2(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y])^2]+2c_1c_2(X-\mathbb{E}[X])(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y]) \\ &=c_1^2\mathbb{V}[X] + c_2^2\mathbb{V}[Y] + 2c_1c_2\text{Cov}(X,Y) \end{align*}

The implication is that when Cov(X,Y)>0(X,Y)>0, 𝕍(X+Y)>𝕍(X)+𝕍(Y)\mathbb{V}(X+Y)>\mathbb{V}(X)+\mathbb{V}(Y).

For example, if portfolio has multiple stocks that tend to move together (Cov), the combined portfolio is riskier. Conversely, the risk decreases if negatively correlated stocks are held together.

5.1.1 Independence

By extension, if XX and YY are independent, Cov(X,Y)=0(X,Y)=0. Then, 𝕍[X+Y]=𝕍[X]+𝕍[Y]\mathbb{V}[X+Y]=\mathbb{V}[X]+\mathbb{V}[Y]

However, zero Covariance does not imply independence.
For example, let random variable XX take three values:

  1. P(X=−1)=13P(X=-1)=\frac{1}{3}

  2. P(X=0)=13P(X=0)=\frac{1}{3}

  3. P(X=1)=13P(X=1)=\frac{1}{3}

Let Y=X2Y=X^2.
We know that YY is dependent on XX.
Calculating covariance,
We have 𝔼[X]=−1+0+13=0\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac{-1+0+1}{3}=0.
Calculating XYXY,

  • X=−1,Y=1→XY=−1X=-1,Y=1\rightarrow XY=-1

  • X=0,Y=0→XY=0X=0,Y=0\rightarrow XY=0

  • X=1,Y=1→XY=1X=1,Y=1\rightarrow XY=1

So 𝔼[XY]=−1+0+13=0\mathbb{E}[XY]=\frac{-1+0+1}{3}=0
Therefore, Cov(X,Y)=𝔼[XY]−𝔼[X]𝔼[Y]=0\begin{align*} \text{Cov}(X,Y)&=\mathbb{E}[XY]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y] \\ &=0 \end{align*}

Takeaway: Covariance only tests for linear relationships. For non-linear cases like Y=X2Y=X^2, Covariance might be 0 even though the variables are dependent on each other.

5.2 Variance-covariance matrix

[𝕍[X]Cov(X,Y)Cov(X,Z)Cov(Y,X)𝕍[Y]Cov(Y,Z)Cov(Z,X)Cov(Z,Y)𝕍[Z]]\begin{bmatrix} \mathbb{V}[X] & \text{Cov}(X, Y) & \text{Cov}(X, Z) \\ \text{Cov}(Y, X) & \mathbb{V}[Y] & \text{Cov}(Y, Z) \\ \text{Cov}(Z, X) & \text{Cov}(Z, Y) & \mathbb{V}[Z] \end{bmatrix}

5.3 Correlation coefficient

ρX,Y=Cov(X,Y)σXσY\rho_{X,Y} = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y} where σX\sigma_X and σY\sigma_Y, the standard deviations of XX and YY, are the normalisation factor.

Thus, we have ρX,Y∈[−1,1]\rho_{X,Y}\in[-1,1], by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

For example, two random variables YY and XX have the strongest linear relationship when Y=XY=X. Then Cov(X,Y)=Cov(X,X)=σX2\text{Cov}(X,Y)=\text{Cov}(X,X)=\sigma_X^2 The correlation coefficient is ρX,Y=σX2σX2=1\rho_{X,Y}=\frac{\sigma_X^2}{\sigma_X^2}=1

5.4 Change of Variables

5.4.1 PMF (Discrete case)

Let XX be a random variable with PMF pX(x)p_X(x).
Let Y=g(X)Y=g(X).
Then the PMF of YY is obtained by summing up all the original xx values that transform into that yy. pY(y)=∑x:g(x)=ypX(x)p_Y(y)=\sum_{x:g(x)=y}p_X(x) Examples:

  • Y=X2:X∈{1,2,3,4,5,6}→Y∈{1,4,9,16,25,26}Y=X^2:X\in\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\rightarrow Y\in \{1,4,9,16,25,26\} pY(16)=P(X=4)=16p_Y(16)=P(X=4)=\frac{1}{6}

  • Y=Xmod⁡3:X∈{1,2,3,4,5,6}→Y∈{0,1,2}Y=X\mod 3: X\in\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\rightarrow Y\in\{0,1,2\} pY(0)=P(X=3)+P(X=6)=16+16=13p_Y(0)=P(X=3) + P(X=6) = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{3}

5.4.2 PDFs (Continuous case, single variable)

Define random variables XX and Y=g(X)Y=g(X), where gg is continuously differentiable and one-to-one.
Then the PDFs fXf_X and fYf_Y satisfy fX(x)=fY(g(x))|dg(x)dx|f_X(x)=f_Y(g(x))\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}g(x)}{\mathrm{d}x}\right| |dg(x)dx|\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}g(x)}{\mathrm{d}x}\right| here is the scaling factor.

Proof. By definition, the Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) is FX(x)=P(X≤x)F_X(x) = P(X \leq x)

Case 1: g(x)g(x) is strictly increasing.
The inequality is preserved: P(X≤x)=P(g(X)≤g(x))=P(Y≤g(x))=FY(g(x))P(X \leq x) = P(g(X) \leq g(x)) = P(Y \leq g(x)) = F_Y(g(x)) So, we have the identity FX(x)=FY(g(x))F_X(x) = F_Y(g(x))

To obtain the PDF, we differentiate both sides with respect to xx.
On the right side, we apply the Chain Rule: ddxFX(x)=ddxFY(g(x))\frac{d}{dx} F_X(x) = \frac{d}{dx} F_Y(g(x)) fX(x)=fY(g(x))⋅g′(x)f_X(x) = f_Y(g(x)) \cdot g'(x) Since gg is increasing, g′(x)>0g'(x) > 0, so this matches our formula.

Case 2: If g(x)g(x) were strictly decreasing, the inequality would flip (X≤x⇔Y≥g(x)X \leq x \iff Y \geq g(x)), leading to a negative sign.
To cover both cases, we take the absolute value of the derivative:

fX(x)=fY(g(x))⋅|dg(x)dx|f_X(x) = f_Y(g(x)) \cdot \left| \frac{dg(x)}{dx} \right|

Intuitive explanation.
In a PDF, since probability is area, the probability mass in a tiny interval of XX must equal the probability mass in the corresponding interval of YY. fX(x)⋅dx=fY(y)⋅dyf_X(x) \cdot dx = f_Y(y) \cdot dy

The relationship between the interval widths (dydy vs dxdx) is determined by the derivative (the slope of the transformation): dydx=g′(x)⟹dy=g′(x)⋅dx\frac{dy}{dx} = g'(x) \implies dy = g'(x) \cdot dx

Substitute dydy back into the area equation: fX(x)⋅dx=fY(y)⋅(g′(x)⋅dx⏟dy)fX(x)=fY(y)⋅g′(x)\begin{align*} f_X(x) \cdot dx &= f_Y(y) \cdot (\underbrace{g'(x) \cdot dx}_{dy}) \\ f_X(x) &= f_Y(y) \cdot g'(x) \end{align*} To ensure the density is positive (even if g(x)g(x) is decreasing), we take the absolute value of the derivative: fX(x)=fY(g(x))⋅|dg(x)dx|f_X(x) = f_Y(g(x)) \cdot \left| \frac{dg(x)}{dx} \right|

Example. Y=aX+b→fX(x)=|a|fY(ax+b)Y=aX+b\rightarrow f_X(x)=|a|f_Y(ax+b).

5.4.3 PDFs (Continuous case, multiple variables)

Let X1,…,XnX_1,\ldots,X_n be nn continuous random variables, transformed to nn new random variables Y1,…,YnY_1,\ldots, Y_n using a set of functions Yi=gi(X1,X2,…,Xn),for i∈[1,…,n]Y_i=g_i(X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_n)\:, \quad \text{for } i \in [1,\ldots,n] We then have a Jacobian matrix of size n×nn\times n. J=(∂y1∂x1⋯∂y1∂xn⋮⋱⋮∂yn∂x1⋯∂yn∂xn)J = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial y_1}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial y_1}{\partial x_n} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial y_n}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial y_n}{\partial x_n} \end{pmatrix} Each row ii tells us how each output YiY_i changes as any of the inputs X1,…,XnX_1,\ldots,X_n change. While in single variable calculus, the derivative is just a single number (the slope), for multivariable calculus, the Jacobian matrix is the collection of all the slopes at once.
Then, the Jacobian determinant replaces g′(x)g'(x) in the 1D case. It tells us how much the volume is transformed.

The joint PDFs fX1,…,Xnf_{X_1,\ldots,X_n} and fY1,…,Ynf_{Y1,\ldots,Y_n} satisfy fX1,…,Xn(x1,…,xn)=fY1,…,Yn(g1(x),…,gn(x))⋅|det⁡J(x1,…,xn)|f_{X_1,\ldots,X_n}(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=f_{Y1,\ldots,Y_n}(g_1(x),\ldots,g_n(x))\cdot |\det J(x_1,\ldots,x_n)| where |det⁡J(x1,…,xn)||\det J(x_1,\ldots,x_n)| represents the absolute value of the determinant of the n×nn\times n Jacobian matrix. This number represents the volume scaling factor of the transformation in nn-dimensional space.

In vector notation, with 𝑿=(X1,…,Xn)\mathbf{X}=(X_1,\ldots,X_n), 𝒀=(Y1,…,Yn)\mathbf{Y}=(Y_1,\ldots,Y_n), φ=(g1,…,gn)\varphi=(g_1,\ldots,g_n), fX(x)=fY(φ(x))|det⁡Jφ(x)|f_X(x)=f_Y(\varphi(x))|\det J_{\varphi}(x)| In terms of fYf_Y, fY(y)=fX(inverse of x)⋅1|det⁡A|f_Y(y)=f_X(\text{inverse of x})\cdot \frac{1}{|\det A|}

Example. Y=AX+b→fX(x)=fY(AX+b)|det⁡A|Y=AX+b \rightarrow f_X(x)=f_Y(AX+b)|\det A|.

5.4.4 Gaussian distribution - Change of variable

Let XX follow the Gaussian distribution N(μ,σ2)N(\mu, \sigma^2), fX(x)=12πσ2e−(x−μ)22σ2.f_X(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}.

Change of variable: Y=aX+bY = aX + b. Then, fX(x)=|a|fY(ax+b)⇔fY(y)=1|a|fX(y−ba)⇔fY(y)=12πa2σ2exp⁡(−(y−(aμ+b))22a2σ2)⇔fY(y)=N(aμ+b,a2σ2)\begin{align*} & f_X(x) = |a| f_Y(ax + b) \\ \iff & f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{|a|} f_X \left( \frac{y - b}{a} \right) \\ \iff & f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi a^2 \sigma^2}} \exp(-\frac{(y - (a\mu + b))^2}{2a^2 \sigma^2}) \\ \iff & f_Y(y) = N(a\mu + b, a^2 \sigma^2) \end{align*}

Takeaway: If we apply a linear transformation to a Gaussian random variable, the result is also a Gaussian distribution.

  • The new mean (aμ+ba\mu + b): The center of the curve moves as expected according to aa and bb.

  • The new variance (a2σ2a^2\sigma^2): The spread is increased by the square of the multiplier.
    Note that the variance is not affected by the shift (bb).

5.4.5 Multivariate Gaussian distribution - Change of variable

Let X1X_1 and X2X_2 independently follow the standard Gaussian distribution N(0,1)N(0,1). fX1(x1)=12πexp⁡(−x22)f_{X_1}(x_1)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(\frac{-x^2}{2}) The joint PDF of (X1,X2X_1,X_2) is fX1,X2(x1,x2)=(12πexp⁡(−x22))(12πexp⁡(−x22))=12πexp⁡(−x12+x222)f_{X_1,X_2}(x_1,x_2)=(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(\frac{-x^2}{2}))(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(\frac{-x^2}{2}))=\frac{1}{2\pi}\exp(-\frac{x_1^2+x_2^2}{2}) Generalising to dd-variate standard Gaussian distribution N(𝟎,Id)N(\mathbf{0},I_d), fX(x)=1(2π)d/2exp⁡(−xdTxd2)f_X(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\exp(-\frac{x_d^Tx_d}{2}) Here, xdTxd=[x1x2⋯xd]⋅[x1x2⋮xd]=x12+x22+⋯+xd2x_d^Tx_d = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & x_2 & \cdots & x_d \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_d \end{bmatrix} = x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \cdots + x_d^2.
Also, the covariance matrix Id=[10⋯001⋯0⋮⋮⋱⋮00⋯1]I_d = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 \end{bmatrix}, meaning the variance of each variable is 1, and there is zero covariance between any of the variables.

the d-variate Gaussian distribution fX(x)f_X(x) is N(μ,Σ)N(\mu, \Sigma), where 𝔼[X]=μ\mathbb{E}[X]=\mu and Σ=AAT\Sigma=AA^T is the variance-covariance matrix.

5.5 Moments

For a random variable XX, the kk-th momemnt is μk=𝔼[Xk]\mu_k=\mathbb{E}[X^k] Each subsequent moment provides more information about the structure of the distribution.
For example,
The first moment tells us about the mean, the second moment tells us about the variance, 𝔼[X]=μ1𝕍[X]=𝔼[X2]−(𝔼[X])2=μ2−μ12\mathbb{E}[X] = \mu_1\quad\mathbb{V}[X] = \mathbb{E}[X^2]-(\mathbb{E}[X])^2=\mu_2-\mu_1^2 The third moment tells us about skewness, the fourth tells us about kurtosis, and so on.

A probability distribution is uniquely specified by its moment generating function (MGF). Note: It is not uniquely specified by its moments.

The moment generating function (MGF) is MX(t)=𝔼[etX]={∑xetxpX(x)(discrete),∫etxfX(x)dx(continuous).M_X(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tX}] = \begin{cases} \displaystyle \sum_{x} e^{tx} p_X(x) & (\text{discrete}), \\[15pt] \displaystyle \int e^{tx} f_X(x) \mathrm{d}x & (\text{continuous}). \end{cases}

We use 𝔼[etX]\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}] because from the taylor expansion of ee, etX=1+tX+(tX)22!+(tX)33!⋯e^{tX}=1+tX+\frac{(tX)^2}{2!}+\frac{(tX)^3}{3!}\cdots Taking expectation, MX(t)=𝔼[etX]=𝔼[1]+𝔼[tX]+𝔼[t2X22!]+⋯=1+t𝔼[X]+t22!𝔼[X2]+t33!𝔼[X3]+⋯\begin{align*} M_X(t)&=\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]=\mathbb{E}[1]+\mathbb{E}[tX]+\mathbb{E}[\frac{t^2X^2}{2!}]+\cdots \\ &= 1 + t\mathbb{E}[X] + \frac{t^2}{2!}\mathbb{E}[X^2] + \frac{t^3}{3!}\mathbb{E}[X^3]+\cdots \end{align*} Therefore, by taking the kk-th derivative, MX(k)(t)=μk+μk+1t+μk+22!t2+μk+33!t3+⋯M_X^{(k)}(t)=\mu_k + \mu_{k+1}t + \frac{\mu_{k+2}}{2!}t^2 + \frac{\mu_{k+3}}{3!}t^3+\cdots and setting t=0t=0, we get the moments μk=MX(k)(0)\mu_k=M_X^{(k)}(0)

5.5.1 Example for Gaussian distribution

For a Standard Normal Distribution (Z∼𝒩(0,1)Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)), the MGF is defined as MZ(t)=𝔼[etZ]=∫−∞∞etz12πe−z22dz=12π∫−∞∞e−12(z2−2tz)dz\begin{align*} M_Z(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tZ}] &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{tz} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{z^2}{2}} \, dz \\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\frac{1}{2}(z^2 - 2tz)} \, dz \end{align*}

We complete the square in the exponent: −12(z2−2tz)=−12(z2−2tz+t2−t2)=−12(z−t)2+t22-\frac{1}{2}(z^2 - 2tz) = -\frac{1}{2}(z^2 - 2tz + t^2 - t^2) = -\frac{1}{2}(z-t)^2 + \frac{t^2}{2}. Substituting this back into the integral: MZ(t)=12π∫−∞∞e−12(z−t)2+t22dz=et22∫−∞∞12πe−12(z−t)2dz⏟=1\begin{align*} M_Z(t) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\frac{1}{2}(z-t)^2 + \frac{t^2}{2}} \, dz \\ &= e^{\frac{t^2}{2}} \underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}(z-t)^2} \, dz}_{=1} \end{align*} The integral is equal to 1 because it represents the total probability of a Normal distribution with mean tt and variance 11. 5.6 Poisson distribution

The Poisson distribution models the number of events occuring in some fixed interval (of time or space), given a constant average rate λ\lambda, independence of events.

For a random variable XX that follow a Poisson distribution, we write X∼Po(λ)X\sim \text{Po}(\lambda)

The Poisson distribution is basically a limit of the Binomial distribution B(n,p)B(n,p).
Recall that for X∼B(n,p)X\sim B(n,p), P(X=k)=(nk)pk(1−p)n−kP(X=k)=\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k} Consider some time interval [0,t0,t] divided into nn tiny parts. Some event occurs on average λ\lambda times in [0,t0,t].
The probability of the event occuring in each of the nn parts is roughly λn\frac{\lambda}{n}.
If nn is sufficiently large, each part can have at most one event.
Taking limit of the Binomial distribution as n→∞n\rightarrow\infty, limn→∞[(nk)⋅(λn)k⋅(1−λn)n−k]\lim\limits_{n\to \infty}\left[\binom{n}{k} \cdot (\frac{\lambda}{n})^k \cdot (1-\frac{\lambda}{n})^{n-k} \right] We get P(X=k)=e−λ⋅λkk!P(X=k)=\frac{e^{-\lambda} \cdot \lambda^k}{k!} This is the probability that some event occurs kk times in [0,t0,t], given that it occurs λ\lambda times on average in [0,t0,t].

The MGF for some Poisson distribution is MX(t)=𝔼[etX]=∑k=0+∞etk⋅e−λ⋅λkk!=e−λ∑k=0+∞etkλkk!=e−λ∑k=0+∞(etλ)kk!=e−λ⋅eet⋅λ=eλ(et−1)\begin{align*} M_X(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]&=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}e^{tk}\cdot\frac{e^{-\lambda} \cdot \lambda^k}{k!}\\ &=e^{-\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{tk}\lambda^k}{k!} \\ &=e^{-\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(e^t\lambda)^k}{k!} \\ &=e^{-\lambda}\cdot e^{e^{t}\cdot \lambda} \\ &=e^{\lambda(e^t-1)} \end{align*}

Taking first and second derivatives of MX(t)M_X(t) and setting t=0t=0, μ1=MX′(0)=λ,μ2=MX″(0)=λ+λ2\mu_1=M'_X(0)=\lambda, \quad \mu_2=M''_X(0)=\lambda + \lambda^2 Then 𝔼[X]=μ1=λ,𝕍[X]=μ2−μ12=λ\mathbb{E}[X]=\mu_1=\lambda, \quad \mathbb{V}[X]=\mu_2-\mu_1^2=\lambda Poisson distribution’s mean and variance are both λ\lambda.
