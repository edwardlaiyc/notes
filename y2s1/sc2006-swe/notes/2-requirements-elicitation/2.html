The most common cause of software project failure is not faulty code, but building the wrong system. Clients and end-users rarely possess a complete, precise, and unambiguous understanding of what they need, often conflating superficial desires with fundamental business requirements.

Requirements Engineering is the disciplined process of discovering, analyzing, documenting, and validating the capabilities and constraints of a proposed software system. It bridges the informal vocabulary of the problem domain with the formal engineering models required for system architecture.

1. The Requirements Engineering Lifecycle

Requirements engineering progresses from high-level project vision to a formal contract:

  1. Project Mission Statement (PMS): A concise executive framing defining three core boundaries:
    • The Problem: The operational challenge, system scope, and explicit non-goals.
    • The People: System stakeholders, development teams, sponsors, and end-user profiles.
    • The Outcome: The verifiable success criteria and strategic business benefits.
  2. Requirements Elicitation: Proactively discovering user needs, operational tasks, and domain rules.
  3. Requirements Analysis & Negotiation: Reconciling conflicts between stakeholder groups, prioritizing features, and evaluating feasibility.
  4. Requirements Specification: Authoring the formal Software Requirements Specification (SRS) and constructing conceptual domain models.
  5. Requirements Validation: Inspecting documents and reviewing prototypes with stakeholders to certify completeness and consistency.

2. Requirements Elicitation Techniques

Different elicitation techniques uncover different types of requirements:

Elicitation Technique Operational Mechanism Key Strengths Critical Limitations Optimal Context
Stakeholder Interviews Structured or semi-structured one-on-one sessions with key actors. Deep exploration of individual workflows and pain points; clarifies ambiguity. Subject to personal bias; users often forget implicit, routine tasks. Initial scope definition; eliciting high-level business goals from domain executives.
Direct Observation (Shadowing) Analysts observe end-users executing their daily workflows in their actual work environment. Discovers implicit workarounds, edge cases, and steps users forget to mention verbally. Time-consuming; users alter behavior when observed (Hawthorne effect). Modernizing or replacing manual legacy business processes.
Facilitated Workshops (JAD) Structured, intensive cross-functional group workshops with users, managers, and architects. Rapidly resolves cross-departmental conflicts and aligns divergent priorities. Can be dominated by vocal participants; high scheduling overhead. Resolving conflicting requirements across multiple competing stakeholder departments.
Document & Legacy Archeology Reviewing existing operating manuals, forms, spreadsheets, and legacy source code. Identifies strict regulatory constraints, business calculation logic, and data schemas. Documentation is often outdated; reflects as-is state rather than desired to-be state. Re-platforming or replacing legacy enterprise software.
Competitive Benchmarking Analyzing market-competing systems and industry standards. Exposes standard customer-expected features and modern UX baselines. May copy competitor flaws; misses unique customer differentiators. Developing greenfield commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) or consumer products.
Prototyping Building mockups, wireframes, or interactive prototypes for user testing. Provides concrete tangible artifacts for feedback; validates complex UI flows early. Stakeholders may mistake prototype for finished software; risks scope creep. Eliciting novel, high-uncertainty user interface requirements.

3. Functional vs. Non-Functional Requirements (FR vs. NFR)

Requirements are partitioned into two fundamental categories:

Functional Requirements (FR)

Define what the system must do. They specify behavioral operations, data manipulations, business rules, inputs, outputs, and external system integrations.

  • Syntax Test: “The system must [verb] [entity] when [event] occurs.”
  • Examples:
    • “The system must calculate and display the total rental fee when a customer returns a video.”
    • “The system must generate an alert email to the silo supervisor if internal humidity exceeds 50%.”

Non-Functional Requirements (NFR)

Define how well the system must execute its functions. They specify quality attributes, environmental constraints, compliance rules, and architectural limits.

  • Syntax Test: “The system must be [quality attribute] [quantified metric].”
  • Key Taxonomy (FURPS+ / ISO 25010):
NFR Category Quality Focus Verifiable Engineering Metric Example
Performance Throughput, computational latency, resource consumption. “The system must return search results within 200 ms under a concurrent load of 1,000 active users.”
Reliability & Availability Continuous operation without failure; fault tolerance. “The system must maintain an uptime of 99.95% (maximum unplanned downtime of 4.38 hours per year).”
Security Confidentiality, integrity, authentication, and auditability. “All financial transactions must be encrypted in transit via TLS 1.3, and passwords hashed using bcrypt (cost factor ≥12\ge 12).”
Usability Learnability, operational efficiency, and error rates. “A trained operator must successfully log a new shipment arrival within 45 seconds on their first attempt without assistance.”
Maintainability Ease of isolating defects, upgrading components, and adapting to change. “Mean Time to Repair (MTTR) a critical production fault must not exceed 2 hours.”
Scalability Handling growth in data volume or transaction throughput. “The system architecture must support a 300% increase in storage volume without requiring hardware database schema restructuring.”

[!WARNING] The Verifiability Invariant: A requirement containing unquantified qualitative adjectives (“fast”, “secure”, “user-friendly”, “robust”) is non-verifiable and invalid. Testers cannot construct an objective Pass/Fail oracle for vague statements. Every NFR must be tied directly to a numerical tolerance threshold.

4. Official Tutorial Problems

Tutorial 1 Q1: Atomizing and verifying requirements for an Inventory and Asset Tracking System

Question: Atomize the requirements for an Inventory and Asset Tracking System and verify that each atomized statement is verifiable:

“The System may be queried. A query may contain a user number or a serial number. If the query contains a user number, all equipment assigned to that user is reported. If the query contains a serial number, the user who is assigned that equipment is reported. If the query does not yield any matching record, an error message is displayed. If a computer is not assigned to any user, the user data display should indicate that the computer is not assigned to anyone.”

Solution:

Heuristics for Atomization:

  1. Express exactly one discrete requirement per statement (atomic).
  2. Write in the active voice with unambiguous subject-predicate relationships ("The system shall...").
  3. Explicitly state the trigger, input, and verifiable system response.
  4. Separate normal operational paths from exception/error conditions.

Atomized and Verifiable Requirements Hierarchy:

  • FR-1.0: Query System by User Identifier
    • FR-1.1: The system shall accept a query containing a valid User Number.
    • FR-1.2: Upon receiving a valid User Number, the system shall retrieve and display a list of all equipment currently assigned to that user, including equipment serial numbers, types, and allocation dates.
  • FR-2.0: Query System by Equipment Serial Number
    • FR-2.1: The system shall accept a query containing a valid Equipment Serial Number.
    • FR-2.2: If the queried serial number corresponds to an assigned item, the system shall display the assigned user’s Full Name, User Number, and Department.
    • FR-2.3: If the queried serial number corresponds to an unassigned computer, the system shall display the status message: "Status: Unassigned".
  • FR-3.0: Exception Handling and Zero-Match Reporting
    • FR-3.1: If an entered User Number does not match any registered user record in the database, the system shall display the error message: "Error: User Number not found".
    • FR-3.2: If an entered Serial Number does not match any inventory record in the database, the system shall display the error message: "Error: Equipment Serial Number not found".

Verifiability Check: Every atomized requirement specifies a discrete input and an exact display output that a tester can evaluate using an objective Pass/Fail test case oracle.

Tutorial 1 Q2: Non-functional requirements categorization and metrics

Question: Classify the following system requirements into the appropriate Non-Functional Requirement (NFR) categories and propose a concrete, verifiable engineering metric for each:

  1. Restricting access to confidential patient medical records.
  2. System behavior during network interruptions.
  3. Database search responsiveness during peak daytime hospital operations.
  4. Database recovery following an abrupt power failure.
  5. System capacity expansion to support three additional regional clinics.

Solution:

Requirement Item Primary NFR Category Verifiable Engineering Metric
1. Restricting access to patient records Security (Confidentiality & Access Control) The system shall enforce Role-Based Access Control (RBAC); unauthorized access attempts must be blocked and logged with 100% detection rate.
2. Behavior during network interruptions Reliability & Fault Tolerance In the event of a local network disconnect, the client terminal shall cache transactions locally and auto-synchronize without data loss within 10 seconds of link restoration.
3. Search responsiveness during peak hours Performance (Response Latency) Under a load of 500 concurrent query requests, 95% of patient record lookups must complete and render in ≤1.5 seconds\le 1.5\text{ seconds}.
4. Database recovery after power failure Maintainability & Recoverability (MTTR) The system shall restore full transactional consistency from journal logs within ≤5 minutes\le 5\text{ minutes} of power restoration, with zero committed transaction data loss.
5. Clinic capacity expansion Scalability The backend architecture shall support adding 3 clinic endpoints (increasing concurrent users by 150) with <10%< 10\% degradation in average response latency without requiring core database re-indexing.
