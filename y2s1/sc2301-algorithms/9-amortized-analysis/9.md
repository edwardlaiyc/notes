# 9. Amortized Analysis

This chapter covers the amortized analysis lecture (Week 9). Some data structures have operations that are usually cheap but **sometimes** very expensive. Worst-case analysis charges every operation the expensive price, which is too pessimistic. **Amortized analysis** looks at the total cost of a whole **sequence** of operations, and spreads it fairly over them.

The lecture gives four methods. They always give the same answer. They differ in how you do the bookkeeping.

---

## 1. Why We Need It

### 1.1 The running example: a growing array

An array has a fixed size in memory. Suppose we keep inserting elements, and we do not know in advance how many there will be. When the array is full, we must:

1. allocate a new, larger array,
2. copy every element into it,
3. then insert the new element.

If we grow the array by just one slot each time, every insertion copies all the elements so far: $1 + 2 + \dots + n = \Theta(n^2)$ for $n$ insertions. That is too slow.

**Table doubling:** when the array is full, make the new array **twice** as large. Now copying happens only when the size reaches 1, 2, 4, 8, ...

### 1.2 Worst case per operation is misleading

With doubling, most insertions cost 1 (just write the element). But an insertion that triggers a resize, when the array holds $n$ elements, costs $n + 1$ (copy $n$, write 1). So the **worst case of one insertion is $\Theta(n)$**. Multiplying, $n$ insertions would cost $O(n^2)$.

That bound is true, but far from tight. The expensive insertions are **rare**: after a resize to size $2n$, the next $n - 1$ insertions are cheap.

![Cost of each insertion with table doubling. Most insertions cost 1. A spike happens at each resize. The flat line is the amortized cost: the total spread evenly.](9-artifacts/doubling-cost-chart.png){width=75%}

### 1.3 What amortized cost means

The **amortized cost** of an operation is the total cost of a sequence of operations, divided fairly among them, **in the worst case over all sequences**.

* It is **not** an average over random inputs. No probability is involved. It is a guarantee: **any** sequence of $n$ operations costs at most $n$ times the amortized cost.
* An individual operation can still be expensive. Only the total is bounded.

---

## 2. Method 1: The Aggregate Method

### 2.1 The idea

Add up the **total** actual cost $T(n)$ of $n$ operations, in the worst case. Then

$$\text{amortized cost per operation} = \frac{T(n)}{n}.$$

### 2.2 Table doubling

Count the two kinds of work separately for $n$ insertions, starting from an array of size 1.

* **Writing new elements:** 1 per insertion, $n$ in total.
* **Copying:** at each resize, the copied elements number 1, 2, 4, ..., up to the largest power of 2 below $n$. This geometric series is less than $2n$.

So $T(n) < n + 2n = 3n = O(n)$, and the amortized cost per insertion is $O(1)$ (at most 3).

Example with 8 insertions: the writes cost 8, and the copies cost $1 + 2 + 4 = 7$, so $T(8) = 15$. The most expensive single insertion (the 5th, which copies 4 elements) costs 5. But on average each costs less than 2.

### 2.3 Spotting the pattern

The same sum appears whenever an expensive step costs about $i$ but happens only at $i = 1, 2, 4, 8, \dots$ (the powers of 2), and every other step costs 1. The expensive steps add up to less than $2n$, the cheap ones to at most $n$, so the total is less than $3n$ and the amortized cost is $O(1)$.

---

## 3. Method 2: The Accounting Method

### 3.1 The idea

Charge each operation a fixed **amortized cost**, which may be more than its actual cost. The extra is saved as **credit** (think of coins in a bank). Later, an expensive operation spends the saved credit to pay for its extra work.

The rule: **the bank balance must never go negative.** Then the total charged is always at least the total actual cost, so the total charge is a valid upper bound.

### 3.2 Table doubling

Charge every insertion **3**:

* 1 pays for writing the new element now.
* 2 are saved as credit.

Why 2? Look at the moment the array doubles from size $m$ to $2m$. Just before that, the last resize made the array size $m$, when it held $m/2$ elements. Since then, $m/2$ new elements were inserted, saving $2 \cdot m/2 = m$ credits. The resize must copy $m$ elements, costing $m$. The saved credit pays for it exactly. The balance never goes negative.

Total charged: $3n$. So $T(n) \le 3n$, and the amortized cost per insertion is $1 + 2 = 3 = O(1)$.

---

## 4. Method 3: The Charging Method

### 4.1 The idea

When an expensive operation happens, **charge its cost back to earlier operations**. Then show that no operation is charged too much in total.

$$\text{amortized cost} = \text{actual cost} - \text{cost charged to past operations} + \text{cost charged to it by future operations}.$$

### 4.2 Table doubling

When the array doubles from $m$ to $2m$, the copying costs $\Theta(m)$. Charge it to the $m/2$ insertions made **since the last doubling**: each is charged $\Theta(1)$, about 2. An insertion is charged only once: the next doubling charges only the insertions after this one.

So each insertion costs 1 for itself plus about 2 charged by a future resize: amortized $1 + 2 = 3 = O(1)$.

![The charging method on 8 insertions. Each insertion pays 1 for itself and is charged 1 more for a future copy. Each resize spends those charges.](9-artifacts/charging-method.png){width=75%}

The accounting method and the charging method are close relatives. Accounting pays **in advance** (save credit, spend it later). Charging bills **afterwards** (the expensive operation sends its bill back to earlier ones).

## 5. Method 4: The Potential Method

### 5.1 The idea

Instead of tracking individual coins, define one number, the **potential** $\Phi$, that depends only on the **current state** of the data structure. Think of it as stored energy: cheap operations raise it, and an expensive operation releases it to pay for itself.

The amortized cost of operation $i$ is

$$\hat{c}_i = c_i + \Delta\Phi = c_i + (\Phi_{\text{after}} - \Phi_{\text{before}}),$$

where $c_i$ is the actual cost.

Add this up over $n$ operations. The changes in $\Phi$ cancel out, except the first and the last:

$$\sum \hat{c}_i = \sum c_i + \Phi_{\text{final}} - \Phi_{\text{initial}}.$$

So if $\Phi_{\text{final}} \ge \Phi_{\text{initial}}$ (the potential never drops below its starting value; usually $\Phi$ starts at 0 and is never negative), then

$$\sum c_i \le \sum \hat{c}_i.$$

The total amortized cost is an upper bound on the total actual cost.

### 5.2 How to choose $\Phi$

Choose $\Phi$ so that:

* it is **low** right after an expensive operation (the expensive operation spends it), and
* it **rises** a little with each cheap operation (saving up for the next expensive one).

### 5.3 Table doubling

Let $n$ be the number of elements and $m$ the capacity. Choose

$$\Phi = 2n - m.$$

Right after a doubling, the array is half full ($n = m/2$), so $\Phi = 0$. As it fills up, $\Phi$ rises to $m$.

**Insertion without a resize.** Actual cost 1. $n$ increases by 1 and $m$ stays the same, so $\Delta\Phi = 2$. Amortized: $1 + 2 = 3$.

**Insertion with a resize.** Before: $n$ elements and $m = n$ (full). Actual cost: copy $n$, write 1, so $n + 1$. After: $n + 1$ elements, capacity $2n$.

$$\Delta\Phi = \big(2(n+1) - 2n\big) - \big(2n - n\big) = 2 - n.$$

Amortized: $(n + 1) + (2 - n) = 3$.

Every insertion has amortized cost 3, so the amortized cost is $O(1)$.

![The potential method on 8 insertions. Each column shows n, m, the potential, the actual cost and the change in potential. Every amortized cost is 3 (the first is 2).](9-artifacts/potential-method.png){width=90%}

The potential method is the most systematic of the four. Once you have $\Phi$, each operation's amortized cost is a short calculation.

## 6. Growing and Shrinking a Table

### 6.1 Why not halve at half full?

Deleting elements leaves the array mostly empty, which wastes memory. A first idea is: halve the array when it becomes half full. But consider an array that is exactly full. Insert one element: it doubles. Delete two: it is half full, so it halves and is full again. Insert, delete, insert, delete, ... each operation can trigger a resize costing $\Theta(n)$. That is $\Theta(n)$ per operation, with no saving.

### 6.2 The fix: halve at a quarter full

Double when the array is full. **Halve when it is only 1/4 full** ($n = m/4$), from $m$ to $m/2$.

After any resize, the array is **half full**. To reach the next resize, you need many operations:

* To double again, you must insert until it is full: at least $m/2$ insertions since the last resize.
* To halve, you must delete until it is a quarter full: more than $m/4$ deletions since the last resize.

**Charging argument.**

* A doubling from $m$ to $2m$ costs $\Theta(m)$. Charge it to the $m/2$ insertions since the last resize: $\Theta(1)$ each.
* A halving from $m$ to $m/2$ costs $\Theta(m)$. Charge it to the more than $m/4$ deletions since the last resize: $\Theta(1)$ each.

Each insertion or deletion is charged only once. So both have amortized cost $O(1)$.

### 6.3 The potential method for any resizing rule

The same idea works for other rules (other fill levels at which to grow or shrink). Choose a potential that is **0 right after a resize** (when the array is at its "ideal" fill level) and grows as the fill level moves away from it, in either direction. For the rule in 6.2, use

$$\Phi = \big|\,2 \cdot (\text{number of elements}) - \text{size}\,\big|.$$

Right after any resize the array is half full, so $\Phi = 0$. Each insertion or deletion changes $\Phi$ by 2. When the array is full ($n = m$), $\Phi = m$, which pays for copying $m$ elements. When it is a quarter full ($n = m/4$), $\Phi = m/2$, which pays for copying $m/4$ elements. In general, check both kinds of operation:

* **No resize:** actual cost $O(1)$, and $\Phi$ changes by a constant. Amortized $O(1)$.
* **A resize:** actual cost is about the number of elements moved. Choose the constant factor in $\Phi$ so that the drop in $\Phi$ (to 0) is at least as large. Amortized $O(1)$.

## 7. The Binary Counter

### 7.1 The setting

A $k$-bit binary counter starts at 0. INCREMENT adds 1. To add 1, flip the trailing 1s (the 1s at the right end) to 0, then flip the first 0 to 1.

* $0111 \to 1000$ flips 4 bits.
* $1000 \to 1001$ flips 1 bit.

The actual cost of an increment is $1 + (\text{number of trailing 1s})$. In the worst case, that is $k$ flips. So a naive bound for $n$ increments is $O(nk)$.

### 7.2 Aggregate method

Look at how often each bit flips over $n$ increments:

* bit 0 (the rightmost) flips every time: $n$ times;
* bit 1 flips every second time: $n/2$ times;
* bit 2 flips every fourth time: $n/4$ times; and so on.

Total flips: $n + n/2 + n/4 + \dots < 2n$. So the amortized cost of an increment is less than 2: $O(1)$.

### 7.3 Potential method

Let $\Phi = c \times (\text{number of 1s in the counter})$. Suppose an increment has $t$ trailing 1s.

* Actual cost: $\Theta(1 + t)$.
* It turns $t$ ones into zeros and one zero into a one: $\Delta\Phi = c(1 - t)$.
* Amortized: $\Theta(1 + t) + c(1 - t) = \Theta(1)$, choosing $c$ to match the constant in the actual cost.

The counter starts at 0 (so $\Phi = 0$) and $\Phi$ is never negative. The bound is valid.

### 7.4 Adding DECREMENT breaks the bound

If the counter also supports DECREMENT, the amortized $O(1)$ bound fails. Put the counter at $0111\dots1$ ($k$ bits). Then alternate:

* INCREMENT: $0111\dots1 \to 1000\dots0$, flips all $k$ bits.
* DECREMENT: $1000\dots0 \to 0111\dots1$, flips all $k$ bits back.

Every operation costs $k$, so $n$ operations cost $\Theta(nk)$. The amortized argument relied on expensive operations being rare. Here an adversary can make **every** operation expensive.

This shows a general point: an amortized bound holds for the operations it was proved for. Adding a new operation can break it.

---

## 8. Moving Cost Between Operations

The potential method can also **shift** cost from one kind of operation to another. For example, in a binary min-heap, INSERT and EXTRACT-MIN both cost $O(\log n)$. Take $\Phi = c \cdot n \log n$ (with $n$ the number of elements):

* INSERT raises $n$ by 1, so $\Phi$ rises by about $c \log n$. Amortized: $O(\log n) + O(\log n) = O(\log n)$.
* EXTRACT-MIN lowers $n$ by 1, so $\Phi$ falls by about $c \log n$. Choosing $c$ to match the actual cost gives an amortized cost of $O(1)$.

This does not make heapsort faster. Heapsort builds the heap without the INSERTs that would have saved up the potential, and the total actual work of $n$ extractions is still $\Theta(n \log n)$. Amortized costs only redistribute the total. They never reduce it.

---

## 9. Common Traps

* Amortized cost is **not** average-case cost. It needs no probability. It bounds the total of **any** sequence in the worst case.
* A single operation can still cost more than its amortized cost.
* Table doubling: worst case per insertion $\Theta(n)$, amortized $O(1)$.
* In the accounting method, the credit must never go negative.
* In the potential method, use $\hat{c} = c + \Phi_{\text{after}} - \Phi_{\text{before}}$, and make sure $\Phi$ never falls below its initial value.
* Shrink at **1/4 full**, not 1/2 full. Halving at 1/2 lets insert-delete pairs trigger a resize every time.
* The binary counter has amortized $O(1)$ increments, but adding DECREMENT allows $\Theta(nk)$ for $n$ operations.
* All four methods give the same amortized bound.
