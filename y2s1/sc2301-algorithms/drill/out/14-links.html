Problems

Problem 1: change the weights, what survives?

A graph has vertices s,a,t and edges sa=2, at=3, st=4. (a) Find its MST and the shortest path from s to t. (b) Replace every weight w by w^2 and redo both. (c) A second graph is a chain s\!-\!x\!-\!y\!-\!t with edge weights 1,1,1 plus a direct edge st=5. Find its shortest path from s to t, then add 10 to every weight and redo it. Does the MST change? (d) State which of the two answers (MST, shortest path) depends only on the order of the weights, and why. (e) What does this say about negative weights?

Answer

(a) Kruskal takes sa (2) and at (3), then rejects st (4). MST =\{sa,at\}, weight 5. Shortest path: the direct edge st, length 4 (the route through a has length 5).

(b) The weights become 4,9,16. The MST is the same, \{sa,at\}. The shortest path changes: the direct edge now costs 16, but s\to a\to t costs 4+9=13.

(c) Original: the chain costs 3, the direct edge 5, so the chain wins. After adding 10 to every weight: the chain costs 3\cdot11=33, the direct edge costs 15, so the direct edge wins. The MST is still the chain (the three light edges), because the order of the weights did not change.

(d) The MST depends only on the order. Kruskal only compares two weights and never adds them, so any strictly increasing change of the weights makes the same accept/reject decisions. A shortest path adds weights along the path, so a change that is not a scaling can make a different path win. (Squaring and adding a constant both break it: adding c punishes paths with many edges.)

Check by brute force: on 2000 random graphs with distinct weights, the MST did not change in any of them under w\to w^2 or w\to w+50 (2000 of 2000). The shortest-path tree from vertex 0 changed in 838 graphs (w^2) and 820 graphs (w+50).

(e) Prim and Kruskal still work when weights are negative, because only the order matters (the AY24/25 S1 paper, Q2(c)). Dijkstra fails, because it relies on a path never getting shorter when you add an edge.

Problem 2: Prim and Dijkstra differ in one line

Vertices 0,1,2,3 and edges 01=2, 12=3, 02=4, 23=3, 13=5. (a) Run Prim from vertex 0. List the tree edges and the total weight. (b) Run Dijkstra from vertex 0. List the tree edges (\pi links) and the total weight. (c) Which single quantity in the update step is different between the two? (d) Which tree is the right answer to which question?

Answer

(a) Take 01 (2). The fringe is 2 (via 4) and 1\to2 is 3, so take 12 (3). Then take 23 (3). Tree =\{01,12,23\}, weight 8.

(b) Distances: d_1=2, d_2=4. Extract 1: d_3=2+5=7; d_2 stays 4 (2+3=5>4). Extract 2: 4+3=7 is not smaller than 7, so \pi_3 stays 1. Tree =\{01,02,13\}, weight 11, and d=(0,2,4,7).

(c) Prim updates a fringe vertex v if the edge is lighter than its key: if w(u,v) < key[v]. Dijkstra updates if the whole path is shorter: if d[u] + w(u,v) < d[v]. The priority queue, the extract-min loop and the \pi array are the same, so both cost O((V+E)\lg V) with a heap.

(d) Prim’s tree has the smallest total weight (8 against 11). Dijkstra’s tree gives every vertex its smallest distance from the source (vertex 2 is at distance 4, but in Prim’s tree it is at distance 2+3=5). Neither tree is best at the other’s goal. BFS is Dijkstra when all weights are 1.

Problem 3: how close is Mergesort to the decision-tree bound?

For n=1,\dots,8 compute (a) the lower bound \lceil\lg n!\rceil, (b) Mergesort’s worst case W(n)=W(\lceil n/2\rceil)+W(\lfloor n/2\rfloor)+n-1 with W(1)=0, (c) Insertion sort’s worst case n(n-1)/2. (d) For which n does Mergesort meet the bound exactly? (e) What does the gap tell you, and what does it not tell you?

Answer

The values, computed by code:

n 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8
\lceil\lg n!\rceil 0 1 3 5 7 10 13 16
Mergesort worst 0 1 3 5 8 11 14 17
Insertion worst 0 1 3 6 10 15 21 28

(d) For n\le4 Mergesort meets the bound exactly (insertion sort only for n\le3). (e) From n=5 the bound is 7 and Mergesort needs 8. The decision-tree bound says no comparison sort can do better than 7. It does not say 7 is reachable. (Not in the lecture: for n=5 a different algorithm, merge insertion, does reach 7.) The gap stays tiny: continuing the table, W(n)-\lceil\lg n!\rceil=0,0,0,0,1,1,1,1,2,3,3,4 for n=1,\dots,12. Both are n\lg n-\Theta(n), which is why \Omega(n\lg n) is tight: Mergesort and Heapsort are asymptotically optimal, while insertion sort (n^2/2) falls away from the bound already at n=4.

Problem 4: heap construction and the bits of n

In a heap of n nodes let the height of a node be the length of the longest downward path (leaves have height 0). (a) Compute the sum of all node heights for n=1,\dots,8 and compare it with n-s(n), where s(n) is the number of 1-bits in the binary form of n. (b) Use the sum to bound the number of levels that constructHeap can sink keys, and so its comparisons. (c) Brute force over all orderings gives the worst-case comparisons of constructHeap for n=1,\dots,8 as 0,1,2,4,6,7,8,11. Compare with 2n-2s(n)-e(n), where e(n) is the number of trailing zero bits of n. (d) What does the formula give for a perfect tree, n=2^k-1? (e) How many comparisons does a decreasing array of 10 keys need?

Answer

(a) The sums are 0,1,1,3,3,4,4,7, and n-s(n)=0,1,1,3,3,4,4,7: equal. Example n=8: the root has height 3, node 1 has height 2, nodes 2 and 3 have height 1, the rest 0, so 3+2+1+1=7=8-1. The identity was checked for every n<200.

(b) fixHeap on a node of height h sinks the key at most h levels. So constructHeap sinks keys at most \sum h=n-s(n)<n levels in total, with at most 2 comparisons per level, so at most 2(n-s(n)) comparisons: linear. That is the exact reason the build is O(n), and the same sum is the sum of the series behind \sum_h \frac n{2^{h+1}}\,h.

(c) 2n-2s(n)-e(n) gives 0,1,2,4,6,7,8,11 for n=1,\dots,8: it matches the brute force in all 8 cases. (The formula is a known result, not in the lecture. It was checked by brute force only up to n=8.)

(d) For n=2^k-1 we have s=k and e=0, so the worst case is 2n-2\lg(n+1). Check n=7: 14-6=8, equal to the brute-force value. The bit count s(n) is the same quantity as the potential \Phi (number of 1s) in the binary-counter analysis on the amortized page. That is an observation, not a proof of a deeper link.

(e) A decreasing array of n=10 is already a max-heap: the build costs only n-1=9 comparisons (Tutorial 4 Q2), the best case, far from the worst case.

Problem 5: Radix sort needs a stable digit sort

Sort [21,12,22,11] by LSD Radix sort: pass 1 on the units digit, pass 2 on the tens digit. (a) Do it with a stable digit sort. (b) Do it again with a digit sort that is correct but outputs equal digits in the reverse of their arrival order. (c) Say exactly what pass 2 needs from pass 1. (d) Which earlier tutorial question builds the bad digit sort?

Answer

(a) Pass 1 (units, stable): 21,11,12,22. Pass 2 (tens, stable): tens 1 keeps 11,12, tens 2 keeps 21,22. Result [11,12,21,22]: sorted.

(b) Pass 1 (units, ties reversed): units 1 arrive as 21,11 and leave as 11,21; units 2 arrive as 12,22 and leave as 22,12. Result [11,21,22,12]. Pass 2 (tens, ties reversed): tens 1 arrive as 11,12 and leave as 12,11; tens 2 arrive as 21,22 and leave as 22,21. Result [12,11,22,21]: the tens are right but the units are in the wrong order. Not sorted. Even an input that is already sorted fails: of the 24 orderings of three keys from \{11,12,21,22\}, all 24 come out wrong.

(c) Pass 2 only looks at the tens digit. Keys with equal tens must stay in the order pass 1 gave them (sorted by units). That is what “stable” means, and it is how information from the earlier pass survives. The correctness proof of Radix sort is an induction on the passes that uses exactly this.

(d) Tutorial 3 Q2: running the last loop of Counting sort forwards still sorts, but it reverses equal keys, so it is not stable. It is a correct sort and a wrong digit sort for Radix sort.

Problem 6: one triangle, four problems

(a) A robot on a 4\times4 grid moves only down or right. Fill in C(i,j), the number of paths from the top-left cell to cell (i,j). (b) Compare C(3,3) with the binomial coefficient \binom63. (c) Run the “distance and count” version of Dijkstra (Tutorial 5 Q2) on the same grid as a unit-weight graph with down/right edges. How many shortest paths reach the far corner? (d) The plain recursion C(i,j)=C(i-1,j)+C(i,j-1) with C=1 on the first row and column: how many calls does it make for C(3,3)? Give the general formula.

Answer

(a)

j=0 1 2 3
i=0 1 1 1 1
i=1 1 2 3 4
i=2 1 3 6 10
i=3 1 4 10 20

(b) C(i,j)=\binom{i+j}{i}, so C(3,3)=\binom63=20. A path is a word of 6 moves with exactly 3 downs, and choosing the places of the downs gives the count. The table is Pascal’s triangle read along its diagonals.

(c) In the grid, every path to (i,j) has exactly i+j edges, so all paths are shortest and the count is the sum of the counts of the two predecessors: the same recurrence, so the answer is 20 (checked by running the algorithm).

(d) The call count F(i,j) satisfies F=1 on the border and F(i,j)=1+F(i-1,j)+F(i,j-1). By induction F(i,j)=2\binom{i+j}{i}-1: the border gives 2\cdot1-1=1, and the step gives 1+(2a-1)+(2b-1)=2(a+b)-1. So C(3,3) costs 39 calls against 16 table cells (the sub-problem graph has only 16 nodes). The calls grow like \binom{2n}{n}\approx 4^n/\sqrt{\pi n}, exponential: the overlap that makes DP worth it. The champagne tower (AY24/25 S2 Q4) has the same shape with a cap and a halving.

Problem 7: alignment is LCS in disguise

In Tutorial 10 Q5, alignment inserts the gap symbol _ into S_1 and S_2 until both have the same length. Each column must hold two equal letters or a gap, and the cost is the number of gaps. The example S_1=\text{ctatg}, S_2=\text{ttaagc} has cost 5. (a) Find the longest common subsequence (Tutorial 10 Q1) of the two strings. (b) Express the alignment cost with |S_1|, |S_2| and the LCS length, and explain why. (c) Check the example. (d) Compare the two recurrences and their sub-problem graphs.

Answer

(a) LCS =\text{tag}, length 3 (t, a, g appear in this order in both strings).

(b) Cost =|S_1|+|S_2|-2\cdot\text{LCS}. The columns with two equal letters form a common subsequence, so at most LCS columns can be matched. Every other letter of S_1 (|S_1|-\text{LCS} of them) needs a gap in S_2 and every other letter of S_2 (|S_2|-\text{LCS}) needs a gap in S_1. Taking the longest common subsequence as the matched columns minimises the gaps.

(c) 5+6-2\cdot3=5: it matches the example. The identity was also checked by dynamic programming on 500 random pairs of short strings over \{a,c,g,t\}.

(d) LCS: L(i,j)=L(i-1,j-1)+1 if the letters are equal, else \max(L(i-1,j),L(i,j-1)). Alignment: A(i,j)=\min\big(A(i-1,j)+1,\ A(i,j-1)+1,\ A(i-1,j-1)\text{ if equal}\big) with A(i,0)=i, A(0,j)=j. Same (n+1)(m+1) nodes and the same three arrows per node, so both take \Theta(nm) time and space. Maximising matches and minimising gaps are the same problem seen from two sides.
